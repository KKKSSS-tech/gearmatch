How to Choose a Camera & Lens Without Regret

The deeper you dig into spec sheets, the more lost you get. This guide — written by a working video professional who has spent years choosing gear for real productions — cuts camera shopping down to the only questions that matter, in the order that matters. By the end, you should have a clear picture of the one camera you actually need.

Last updated: September 3, 2026

The short version: subject first, then glass

The most important question in camera shopping is not “full-frame or APS-C?” and not “how many megapixels?” It is: what do you want to photograph, and in what situations? A kids’ sports day, tabletop product shots in a dim café, and travel landscapes each point to completely different gear.

One more thing. Image quality is decided more by the lens than by the body. Bodies are replaced every few years; a good lens stays useful for a decade. On a limited budget, the classic play is: buy last year’s body (or used) and spend the savings on glass.

What sensor size actually means

Bigger is not automatically better — every step up is a trade-off.

  • Full-frame — the biggest bokeh and the best low-light performance, in exchange for larger, heavier, pricier bodies and lenses. The default choice for serious work and dark scenes.
  • APS-C — the balance point of image quality, price and portability. The 1.5× crop makes a 200mm lens frame like a 300mm — which is actually an advantage for birds and sports.
  • Micro Four Thirds — the lightest full system, with a 2× crop and often superb in-body stabilization. Great for travel and video; gives up some bokeh and high-ISO headroom to full-frame.
  • Medium format — beyond full-frame in tonality and resolution, but the price, weight and slower operation put it in commercial-studio territory.

Remember: the light camera that is always in your bag takes better pictures than the heavy one that stays home.

Photo, video, or both?

If photography comes first

  • Viewfinder quality (LCDs wash out in bright sun)
  • Autofocus — especially eye-detection AF
  • Burst speed and buffer depth if you shoot action

If video comes first

  • Real-world stabilization (critical if you shoot while walking)
  • 4K crop factor, recording limits and overheating behavior
  • A flip-out screen for vlogging and low angles
  • Microphone and headphone jacks
  • Log profiles and 10-bit recording if you plan to color-grade

If you shoot both, start from the video-strong models — recent hybrid bodies are almost always excellent still cameras too.

Lenses by subject

  • Portraits — a fast 50–85mm prime (f/1.8 or brighter). Backgrounds melt away and your subject pops. Every mount’s “nifty fifty” is a legendary value.
  • Landscapes & cities — a wide zoom (around 16–35mm) with corner-to-corner sharpness and good flare resistance for sunrise and sunset.
  • Birds, aircraft, sports — focal length is king: 300mm minimum, 400mm+ preferred, with fast AF. A telephoto zoom is the sane starting point.
  • Astro — a bright wide prime (f/2.8 or faster, ideally f/2). Aperture beats stabilization here, and a tripod is effectively mandatory.
  • Vlogging — light weight, strong stabilization, and a wide view (about 24mm equivalent). At arm’s length, the frame is tighter than you expect.
  • Macro & product — a true 1:1 “macro” lens. A 90–105mm macro doubles beautifully as a portrait lens.

Reading a lens name in 3 minutes

  • Focal length (mm) — smaller numbers see wider, bigger numbers reach further. Zooms show a range (24–70mm); primes show one number (50mm).
  • F-number — smaller means a “faster” lens: better in the dark, bigger bokeh. A constant f/2.8 zoom costs more but never lets you down at night.
  • Stabilization — every brand names it differently (IS / OSS / VR / OIS). If the body has in-body stabilization, an unstabilized lens is less of a problem.
  • Prime vs zoom — start with a zoom to learn which focal lengths you actually use, then add a prime there. That order rarely fails.

Budget strategy and buying used

Start with the complete kit, not a fixed body-to-lens ratio. Reserve money for the lens your subject needs, a memory card, spare power and carrying equipment, then compare bodies with the amount left. Our worked planning example (Japanese) separates those costs; it is an illustration, not a live price quote.

For used gear, read the individual condition report, warranty and return terms. Grade names are not interchangeable between retailers, and a grade alone does not establish the condition of a particular item:

  • Cosmetic condition — compare the actual item photos and the retailer's definition of its grade.
  • Operation — check the listed tests, defects and included accessories; appearance does not prove function.
  • After purchase — confirm the warranty, return window and who pays return shipping.

Five classic mistakes

  1. Buying megapixels — 20MP is plenty for social media and 4K video. High-resolution sensors also punish handshake and fill drives fast.
  2. Spending everything on the body — and living with the kit lens. Glass is where image quality lives.
  3. Ignoring weight — “feels fine” in the shop is not “feels fine” after a full day. Think total weight with the lens mounted.
  4. Choosing a mount without checking the lens lineup — your lenses lock you into the mount. Confirm the system is strong in the genres you shoot.
  5. Paying launch price in launch week — the outgoing model at its post-announcement discount is usually the best value in the entire market.
